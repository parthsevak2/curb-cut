Curb Cut is for anyone who has something at work that's quietly hard, and who has never asked for help with it. A chair that hurts by three o'clock. A light that brings on a headache by lunch. A meeting you keep missing half of. Most people work out what it would cost to ask, and say nothing, because asking means explaining your body to the person who decides what you're worth.

Curb Cut is an Agentforce agent that's on the worker's side. You tell it what's getting in the way, in your own words, and you never have to say why. It shows you what other people have asked for, drawn from a library of real accommodations that each carry the source they came from, along with what each one usually costs. If you want to ask, it writes the request in your words and reads it back, and nothing is sent until you clearly say yes. A hedge is refused, and a test proves the refusal. If typing is the hard part, you can send a photo. If you sign, you can send a video, and a person interprets it, never a machine.

Here's the size of it. Among college-educated people in white-collar jobs in the United States, thirty in a hundred have a disability under the federal definition. Three tell their employer. Of the people who did tell, most say it got them better support. So it works for nearly everyone who tries, and almost nobody tries. The answer, when people ask, is usually yes and usually free: of the 1,425 employers who gave the Job Accommodation Network cost figures through 2024, 61% said the accommodation cost them nothing. The money was never the barrier. The asking was.

Three things make Curb Cut different. You can ask before telling anyone anything, because nothing is written down while you're anonymous. There's no field for a diagnosis, anywhere: it isn't encrypted or hidden behind a permission, it simply doesn't exist in 61 fields across 9 objects, and a build check fails if anyone adds one. If you name a condition anyway, it's taken out before anything is stored, and you're told so in the same reply. And nobody is ever routed to a telephone, because for some of the people this serves, a phone call is a door that doesn't open.

Six doors share one Apex router: web, voice, text, email, Slack and MCP. Web, voice, text, email and MCP are live today; Slack is built and waiting for a workspace. Two of those doors work on a basic phone with no internet at all. It also ships with a console for the person on the other end, because a promise that a real human will pick this up is worth nothing unless that human has somewhere to stand.
