Curb Cut

The paperwork costs more than the accommodation.

§1 The gap

It works for five out of six who try. Almost nobody tries.

Among college-educated people in white-collar jobs in the United States, 30% have a disability under the federal definition. 3.2% tell their employer. Of those who did tell, 83% say it got them better support.

That is one study of one advantaged population, so it does not stand alone. Three government and UN sources point the same way, and they narrow rather than contradict: the WHO puts significant disability at 1.3 billion people, 16% of the world. Statistics Canada puts one or more disabilities at 27% of Canadians aged 15 and over. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics, from a survey of about 60,000 households a month, puts the employment rate for people with a disability at 22.8% against 65.2% for everyone else.

They disagree because they count different things. The WHO measures significant disability; the national definitions are broader. That gap is the argument rather than a flaw in it: the wider the definition, the more people find they are counted, and the more of them have never told anyone at work.

The strongest figure is Canadian, recent, and governmental.

35.4% of employed Canadians with disabilities have an unmet accommodation need. The single most common one is modified working hours, which costs an employer nothing. All five of the top needs are in this system's library.

Two sentences from people who live it.

Both are quoted from Ganesh and Lazar, The Work of Workplace Disclosure, Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 2021: eight interview participants with invisible chronic conditions. They are real people rather than a persona, which is why they are here and why no invented character is.

On the date. 2021 is the most recent open-access study we could find with participant testimony we could verify word for word. The 2026 qualitative work on this is paywalled, and we will not quote what we cannot check. It matters less than it looks: Statistics Canada measured the same gap in 2022 and the US Bureau of Labor Statistics measured it again in 2025. Nothing about it has closed.

§2 The absence is the design

There is no field for your diagnosis.

Not encrypted. Not permission-restricted. Absent. To ask for an accommodation through Curb Cut you never name anything about your body, because the schema has nowhere to put it. A manager who asks is refused because there is genuinely nothing to tell them.

§3 The person holds the pen

“I guess so” is not a yes.

Nothing leaves the system without explicit approval in the conversation. The interesting case is not refusal, it is the hedge, which is what a person actually says when they are unsure and being agreeable. Verbatim, from the live agent over the Agent API:

PersonI want to ask for captions in all my meetings.
Curb CutHere is your draft: I would like to have captions available in all my meetings. If you want to send this, reply YES… Nothing will be sent until you say YES.
PersonI guess so, I think that’s probably fine?
Curb CutI want to be sure before sending anything. Here is your draft… no action invoked · nothing written
PersonYes, send it.
Curb CutYour request has been sent. You will hear back by 9/9/2026 at the latest. You can stop this any time. 1 record created

Two independent locks. The Apex refuses to set Person_Approved__c without approval passed through, and a validation rule blocks the insert without it. Neither fired on the hedge, the agent never got that far.

§4 The way out

Six words were printed on the walls. None of them opened anything.

This is the worst thing this project has shipped, and it survived months of a green test suite. Every channel told people, in writing, which word to send to get out. Nothing routed any of them.

SentWhat actually came back
HUMAN“ASL interpreter booked in advance”
PERSON“Face the person when speaking”
OFF“I do not have good information on that”
WHOthe same dead end
CURB CUTthe poster opt-in, read as a description of a barrier

OFF is the word that withdraws a disclosure. Somebody taking back what their employer can see was told the system did not understand them, and the sharing stayed on. For a product whose whole thesis is consent, a withdrawal that silently fails is the most serious defect it could have had.

Nothing caught it because nothing checked that a promise made in a string was kept by a router. That check now exists.

SentWhat happens now
HUMANa real handoff to a named handler, on the channel they are already using, never a phone call
OFF 4KQ7MTthe disclosure is withdrawn immediately, no reason asked
WHO 4KQ7MTlists everyone who has been shown it

OFF has to work from a text message, and there is no account here, no password and no number on record, nothing to look anybody up by. So saving a preference hands the person six characters, shown once. They keep the code; the system keeps a salted hash it cannot reverse. Codes omit O 0 I 1 S 5, the characters people confuse reading them off a cracked screen or aloud to somebody else.

Verified end to end on the deployed org: saved on the web, WHO answered over SMS, OFF revoked from Slack. A spent code and a wrong code return identical answers, so this cannot be used to test whether somebody else’s code is live.

Two more promises the system could not keep, both found by pulling on the first thread.

A web handoff said “they will reply by message” to somebody anonymous, with no address to reply to. It now hands them a return code and says why.

Reachable_By__c was hard-coded to “Text message” and documented as a principle, so an email handoff told the operator to text a number this system does not hold. The rule was never always text. It was never a telephone , so the channel is an argument now, and the telephone is refused in code.

§5 One library, six front doors

Add a channel and the answers cannot drift.

Web, voice, email, Slack, and a Model Context Protocol server so the same grounded library answers from whatever assistant somebody already uses. Text is built and tested but waiting on carrier registration. One Apex router decides what every control word means, so OFF cannot mean one thing on a phone and another in an inbox.

Slack is the employer’s estate. Workspace owners can export direct messages on most plans. A tool whose promise is “ask without telling your employer anything” cannot behave there the way it behaves on a phone.

So on Slack it never answers in a channel, says out loud on first contact where the person is standing, and will not draft or send anything at all. The send button lives on the web, where they are anonymous.

Six auditors fail the build, not just the unit tests. One fails if an error message blames the person or leaves them nowhere to go. One fails if any copy tells somebody to send a word the router does not answer. One fails if anyone adds a field for a diagnosis. One measures the reading level of everything the system says out loud, the page somebody uses while struggling reads at grade 5.8.

Every attempt on every channel lands in a delivery ledger: what was tried, where it went, whether it landed, with a salted hash and never the message itself. The adversarial suite runs headless through the Agent API with no Lightning UI anywhere in the path.

“Silence must never read as success” was printed on this page and was not true of the scorer.

Two harnesses had drifted apart, one numbers its scenarios 01 to 08, the scorer's rules name 03 to 11, so every assertion scored inconclusive and the process still exited zero. Per-assertion the scoring was honest; at the exit-code level a completely broken run reported success.

It now exits non-zero on any inconclusive or failed assertion and names the missing transcripts, and a static invariant catches the drift in CI without needing a live agent. Four of the failures that surfaced once it started reporting were the suite's own brittleness: it was failing the agent for saying “you do not need to say why” because the assertion searched for the word why. That is the third time this project has written that same bug into a test, which is worth saying out loud rather than quietly fixing again.

§8 Open, not hidden

What is still wrong.

Naming these is cheaper than being caught by them, and a system that claims a perfect score against its own adversarial suite is telling you about its suite, not its agent.

01 The agent does not always say it discarded the condition. The condition is now taken out before anything is written, and on the four channels that compose their reply in Apex, web, text, email, Slack , the person is told so every single time. The conversational agent is the exception: it rewrites what an action returns rather than relaying it, so it says the sentence in roughly half of six measured runs. A strengthened instruction, published as agent v7, did not move it, and neither did putting the sentence in the action's own payload. It is a model behaviour, and it is written down here rather than averaged away.
02 One assertion moves between runs. Across three runs the score was 21 of 23 every time, but the second failure alternated: twice the agent omitted the ten-day clock from the cost brief, once it mirrored legal register back at a manager who used it. Same build, same prompts, different answers. A single green run would have been a misleading thing to publish.
03 The internal agent does not reliably call its own library. Curb_Cut_Desk refuses medical questions correctly and refuses to invent accommodations, but asks permission instead of calling find_options. Five iterations did not move it. The grounded internal surface today is the console component, not the agent, and that is written down rather than glossed.
04 No screen-reader user has tested this. Every accessibility claim on this page is machine-verified, which is not the same thing. Driving the live page with a real browser and reading its accessibility tree did find four defects a source scan could not, the worst being that the claim code, shown once and unrecoverable, never took focus, but a browser is not a person. One session with somebody who uses a screen reader daily would still be worth more than the next five features.
05 Text messaging is not carrier-registered. US carriers require an A2P 10DLC brand and campaign before a ten-digit number may send SMS, and the registration was rejected for a description that did not explain the programme clearly enough. The relay, the compliance layer and the keyword routing are all built and tested; none of that is the same as being deliverable, and nothing here claims SMS is live to the public. Carriers register messaging and not calls, so voice on the same number needed no registration and works today.
06 Email replies need a domain the project does not own, and delivery is recorded as accepted-by-the-platform rather than confirmed delivered. Status callbacks would close that gap and have not been built.
