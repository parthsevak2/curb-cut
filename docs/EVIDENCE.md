# Evidence

Every figure Curb Cut states in public, with its source, its population, its
date, and what it does not say. Nothing here is asserted from memory.

Read the population line before quoting a number. Most disagreements about
disability statistics are really disagreements about who was counted.

---

## The layered picture, widest to narrowest

Four sources, four different definitions of disability, four different
populations. They do not contradict each other. They narrow.

| Level | Figure | Population and definition | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global | **1.3 billion people, 16%** | World population with *significant* disability | WHO, *Global Report on Health Equity for Persons with Disabilities*, 2022 |
| Canada | **27%** | Canadians aged 15+, one or more disabilities | Statistics Canada, *Canadian Survey on Disability*, 2022 |
| United States | **22.8% employed** vs 65.2% without a disability | All people with a disability, 16+ | US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025, from the Current Population Survey (~60,000 households monthly) |
| US white-collar | **30%** | College-educated, full-time, white-collar, aged 21–65, broad federal definition | Coqual (formerly Center for Talent Innovation), *Disabilities and Inclusion* |

**Why the numbers differ.** WHO counts *significant* disability, which is the
narrowest lens and gives the lowest figure. Statistics Canada and the US federal
definition are broader and include conditions many people would not name out
loud. The Coqual 30% is highest because it uses the broad federal definition
*and* asks a population that is often assumed to have no disabilities at all.

That gap between 16% and 30% is not sloppiness. It is the whole point: the
broader the definition, the more people discover they are being counted, and the
more of them have never told anyone at work.

## 1. The disclosure gap

| Figure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| US college-educated white-collar employees 21–65 with a disability | **30%** | Coqual, *Disabilities and Inclusion* |
| Of those, share who self-identify to their employer | **3.2%** | same |
| Share whose disability is invisible | 62% | same |
| Of those who did disclose, felt it improved their support | **83%** | same |

**Source, precisely.** Coqual is an independent research nonprofit, formerly the
Center for Talent Innovation. This is a multinational study; the 30% and 3.2%
are the US figures. The finding is corroborated in the trade and academic press.

**What it does not say.** This is not a government statistic and it is not a
census. It is one well-cited study of one advantaged population, and it is
roughly a decade old. We use it because it is the only figure that measures the
*disclosure* gap directly, and because the government data below points the same
way. We would replace it tomorrow with a national statistics office equivalent
if one existed.

**Why we lead with it anyway.** Disclosure overwhelmingly helps the people who
do it, and almost nobody does it. The barrier is not the outcome. It is the act
of telling somebody.

## 1a. Why this needs arguing at all

It should not take a dataset to establish that a person is worth an hour of
flexibility. It does, so here is the dataset. But the argument underneath it is
not statistical:

- **One roadblock is not one bad day.** Nobody is dismissed for needing an hour
  moved. They stop being considered, stop being staffed, stop being asked.
- **It compounds.** A job lost is a reference lost, a gap on a record, a mortgage
  refused. The roadblock lasts an afternoon. The consequence lasts a decade.
- **It is almost always man-made.** Not the condition. The form, the login, the
  policy nobody can find, the manager who wants proof.
- **It reaches past the person.** Their family, their children, and the income
  that would have paid for the thing that helped.

We say everyone is equal. In a market, everyone is a transaction, and the people
this affects are consistently priced as though they bring less. They are not
abstractions. They are colleagues, friends, and members of the same communities
as the people writing the policies.

The rest of this document exists because that argument, on its own, has not been
enough.

## 1b. What it costs the person, in their own words

Peer-reviewed, quotable, and attributable. We use real testimony rather than a
composite persona, because a made-up person on a conference stage is the one
thing that would deserve to sink this.

| Quote | Speaker |
|---|---|
| "I wish we had a percent sick number on our forehead so others could have something tangible to understand what we are going through" | Participant F-206 |
| "having to sit down and explain it six times a day to 20 different people adds to that exhaustion" | Participant P1 |

**Source.** Ganesh, S. and Lazar, A., *The Work of Workplace Disclosure: Invisible
Chronic Conditions and Opportunities for Design*, Proceedings of the ACM on
Human-Computer Interaction, 2021. Eight interview participants with invisible
chronic conditions, plus analysed forum posts.
<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297988/>

**On the date.** 2021 is the most recent open-access study we could find with
participant testimony verifiable word for word. The 2026 qualitative work on
workplace disclosure is paywalled, and we will not quote what we cannot check.
The age matters less than it appears: Statistics Canada measured the same gap in
2022, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics measured it again in 2025, and it has not
closed. The testimony describes a condition the government data confirms is
current.

**Why these two.** The first is what a person actually wants: to be believed
without having to prove it. The second is the exact burden the standing
preference exists to remove, and it is the reason "say it once" is a feature
rather than a convenience.

**What we do not do.** We do not put photographs of disabled people in the deck.
Stock photography of disability is a well-documented trope that the people it
depicts consistently object to, and we have no consent from anyone real. Large,
quiet typography of a real sentence carries more than a posed picture, and costs
nobody their likeness.

## 1a. It is not a white-collar problem

The disclosure study above asked white-collar workers because that is where the
question had never been asked. The national statistics say who actually holds
the jobs.

**What this says.** The people this affects are more likely to be paid by the
hour, to work part time, and to hold service, production and transport jobs
than to sit at a desk. That is the shift worker, the cleaner, the warehouse
picker, the minimum-wage hire with no work email and no HR desk. Nobody has
measured how many of them tell their employer. The only study that measured
telling at all asked white-collar workers and found three in a hundred.

**Why Curb Cut's doors look the way they do.** Text and voice on a basic phone,
no work login, no account, and never a telephone call back: those are the
doors a person on a shift can use from a break room.

## 2. The accommodation gap, from a national statistics office

This is the strongest evidence in the project, and it is government data.

| Figure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Employed Canadians with disabilities who needed a workplace accommodation | **35.4%** | Statistics Canada, *Canadian Survey on Disability*, 2022 |
| Employed Canadians with disabilities with an **unmet** accommodation need | **35.4%** | same |
| Most common need: **modified work hours** | 16.3% | same |
| Then modified or different duties | 11.6% | same |
| Then working from home | 10.9% | same |
| Then modified or ergonomic workstation | 10.7% | same |
| Then a special chair or back support | 10.3% | same |

**Why this matters more than it looks.** The single most common unmet need in
Canada is *modified working hours*. It costs an employer nothing. It requires no
equipment, no budget line and no procurement. It requires somebody to say yes.

Every one of the top five needs is in Curb Cut's library.

## 3. What accommodations actually cost

| Figure | Value |
|---|---|
| Employers surveyed | 26,028 |
| Employers who responded | 5,406 |
| Respondents who gave cost information | 1,425 (26% of respondents) |
| **Of those 1,425: cost nothing** | **61%** |
| One-time cost | 33%, median **$300** |
| Ongoing cost | 6%, median **$2,400 per year** |
| Very or extremely effective | 66% of 2,069 answering |
| **Ineffective** | **12%** |

**Source.** Job Accommodation Network, *Costs and Benefits of Accommodations*,
employer survey through 2024. <https://askjan.org/topics/costs.cfm>

**The denominator matters.** The 61% is of the 1,425 employers who gave cost
figures, not of all 5,406 who responded. Anyone quoting "61% are free" without
that qualifier is overselling it, and so would we be.

**We also publish the 12%.** Twelve percent of accommodations were reported
ineffective. A page that only quotes the 66% is selling something.

## 4. Why now: the asymmetry

| Figure | Value | Source and exact standing |
|---|---|---|
| Employers using some automated tool to screen or rank candidates | "as many as **83%**" | An estimate quoted at the EEOC public hearing *Navigating Employment Discrimination in AI and Automated Systems*, 31 January 2023 |
| Fortune 500 companies doing the same | "up to **99%**" | same hearing, same standing |

**Be careful with this one.** It is an estimate quoted at a hearing, not an EEOC
finding, and we say so wherever we use it. It is directional, not precise.

The argument it supports does not depend on the exact number: one side of the
accommodation conversation has been automated at scale, and the other side is a
person on a hard day with a PDF.
